# Onward — Chapter One: The Road That Waits

## I. The Dark Answers

Begin at dusk, the day the vault was opened. Most of this age's beginnings will be at dusk; it is when the world does its remembering, and this is an age of remembering.

The hamlet felt the opening before it heard the news. Small ways first, as always. The lanterns along the lanes took their murmured feedings eagerly that evening, the flames standing up gold on the first breath instead of the second. The wellspring came up brighter than the season warranted, gold rising through it in threads thick as wool-yarn, and the children stayed at its rim past their suppers, telling it what they wanted most with unusual conviction. At the inn the fire leaned toward the door whenever it opened. Berry said afterward she thought a storm was coming. She was righter than most guesses are: something was moving in the low seams of the world, and the hearth knew its own.

What was moving was this. A gate that had been shut beyond living memory stood open, and the lines sealed behind it — dimmed, thinned, gone strange in the long dark, but not gone — were out under the sky for the first time in ages. And they were doing what every kept thing does, what the water does, what the Glow has always done.

They were trying to come home.

At the south gate, Gatewarden Alder set the bar as he has set it every dusk of his office, and before it dropped he asked the dark road his question, as the office has asked it time out of mind: *Who goes, and what do you carry?* And he waited his three breaths, out of courtesy, in case the dark should finally answer.

On the first breath, nothing, as there had been nothing for thirty years of asking.

On the second breath, far down the road where the sure ground gives out and the grey begins, a small light rose. It came on slowly, wavering, the way a moth comes — drawn, not aimed — a glimmer no bigger than a cupped palm, pale as a star seen through cloth. It came up the road as if the road were the only thing in the world it was sure of, and it reached the gate-lamp that hangs above the bar, the one the taper-someone murmurs over nightly, and it pressed itself to the glass the way cold hands press to a fire.

And the gate-lamp's flame stood up. Gold, with morning in it. The way a door opens for someone long expected.

On the third breath the little light went on — over the bar, past Alder close enough that he felt it the way you feel a word on the edge of recall — up the lane, between the lantern-pools, to the wellspring. The children had gone in by then. Only the water saw it arrive. The water is patient, and it remembers with you, and what rose bright in the pool that night went down brighter.

Alder stood at his gate a long while. He is a formal man; the office came down to him with its habits whole, and he has kept them the way you keep what you are given, without asking it questions. Thirty years he had asked the dark out of courtesy. It had never once been discourteous of the dark, he felt, to say nothing back. But there is a difference, he discovered that night, between a courtesy and a hope; the difference is what it does to you when it is answered.

The dark did not speak. The dark arrived.

At first light he came up the lane to find the Keeper, and his knock was the knock of a man who has rehearsed a sentence all night and knows it will come out plain anyway.

"Keeper," he said. "The gate was answered."

> *Entered: one small light, at the south gate, answering the question. Went on to the water. Weather clear. —C.*

## II. The Barred Road

By midmorning half the hamlet had walked out to look at the road, which tells you how long it had been since the road was news.

It runs sure for the first mile — south past the field-gates, under the big beeches — and it runs sure because it is used: feet on it daily, carts weekly, the way spoken by boot-soles if by nothing else. A road, like anything true, holds exactly as well as it is held. Nobody in the hamlet would have put it that way. They put it *the near road's fine, it's the far road that ends.*

And at the end of the sure mile, there it was, as it had been all their lives: the barrier.

Understand what stood there. Not a deadfall, not a tangle of thorn, not the grey itself. A fence. A good one — post and rail, true-set, whitewashed every spring, with a bench beside it facing homeward and a border of tended sweet-william at its feet. A barrier maintained with love. Mark that, for it is worth a whole vault of learning: love is how you know the barriers that will be hardest to take down.

The fence was Old Hollis's, as the whitewash was, as the sweet-william was. He was there before the crowd arrived, as if he had felt the hamlet's attention swing south in his sleep. He stood at his fence the way the fence stood at the road.

"The road ends here," said Old Hollis, "and a mercy it does."

Now, the hamlet held Hollis dear, and held his fence the way you hold an old custom — half believed, half just *there*, the furniture of the world. The road ends. Everyone knew the road ends. Children grew up knowing it the way they knew the wood was deep and the winter long. Only that morning there was a wisp of an answer in everyone's memory, come up that very road out of the country where roads end; and a thing everyone knows does not survive contradiction half as well as a thing everyone has checked.

It was Tamsin who said so. Tamsin the drover — you will want to know her, so look once and look properly: brown and broad-shouldered and boot-worn, a droving-goad through her belt like other folk wear a knife, and a voice that carries to the back of a herd without rising. She had droved the near loop for years — fields and back, fields and back, the one mile the world had left her — with her cart and her mule Barley, who is in this tale on merit and will tolerate no less. Her grandmother had droved the long roads when there were long roads: away weeks, home singing. Tamsin had the songs and the mile. You may judge for yourself which of the two she counted her inheritance.

"Something walked in off the far road last night," Tamsin said, "and it's the first thing in thirty years that's come *this* way up it. I mean to see what the road says to that. Hollis — I'm asking you for the stile."

"And I'm telling you what I've told every asking these thirty years," said Hollis, and his voice was not unkind, which made it worse. "The road ends here. What's past my fence isn't road. It's the grey wearing a road's shape, and it does not give back what walks into it."

He believed it entirely. That is the thing to carry out of this scene. Nobody in this chapter is lying to anyone. It would all be so much easier if somebody were.

## III. The Fable of the Waywright

That evening the Gilded Acorn was full, the way a tavern fills when the hamlet has a thing to decide and no one has called a meeting. And Berry — who keeps the hearth, and keeps the pot, and keeps, though she would laugh at you for saying it, a good deal else — waited until the arguing had worn itself to a hum, and wiped her hands, and said, "Well. My grandmother had a tale about roads."

You know what a fable is, if you have heard the First Telling: a truth that rode out the ages hidden inside a story, kept breathing by the pleasure of the telling. The hamlet did not know they were being handed one. Berry maybe did. With Berry it is safest to assume.

Here is the fable as she told it.

---

*In the years when lanterns were new, the light pooled where the lamps were, and the dark pooled between. Folk went lamp to lamp like stones across a stream, and the ways between the lamps — the long stretches, the middles — began to be lost. For a road is a line like any true thing: a line the land holds with you, and only so long as you hold your half.*

*Now in those years there lived a stone-cutter who could not carry a tune in a bucket, and — which was worse for a walker — could not carry a way in her head. Songs would not stay with her; turnings ran out of her like water through fingers. Three times she was lost within sight of her own chimney-smoke. A keeper of that age would have said: hold the way-line, say it as you walk. She could not. Some heads are built so, and hers was one, and she was ashamed of it in the usual useless fashion, until the day she stopped being ashamed and got out her chisels.*

*If a line would not stay in her head, she reasoned, it could stay in something harder.*

*She cut the first waystone on the road she was most lost on, which was her own. A standing stone, breast-high, and in its face she cut the stretch — plainly, deeply, the way you say a thing to someone you need to hear it: where this road has come from, and where it is going, and how it goes. And she found what the Clerk's kind have always known and walkers had not yet learned: the stone did not forget. Rain did not argue with it. Years did not wear it as they wear a memory. The line slept in the stone the way words sleep on a page — which is to say it did not live there, for stone has no breath; it* waited *there.*

*"But a waiting line is not a kept one," said a keeper of the old sort, frowning at the stone. "Stone cannot speak."*

*"No," said the stone-cutter. "But any fool walking past can read."*

*And that was the craft, whole, in one line, though it took a lifetime to plant it down all the roads of that country: the stone keeps the stretch, and the walker feeds the stone. You read the way aloud as you passed — going out, coming home, every walker a feeding — and so the road was spoken daily by everyone who used it and burdened no one who did. The ways between the lamps came back. Drovers learned the stones' lines into their droving-songs, so the long stretches could be sung where stones were far between. And the stone-cutter, who never did learn to hold a way in her head, was named for the work, as the custom was: the Waywright. Hear the difference in the two gate-crafts and you have them both: a gate asks who goes. A stone remembers where.*

*They say a young keeper asked her once, near the end, why stone — why not simply teach the songs, which weigh nothing and want no chisel. And the Waywright, who had spent a life being the person songs would not stay with, said: "Songs die with singers, if the singers are careless. Stone waits. Cut it once, and it will wait a hundred years for one walker with the wit to read it aloud. I have met the walkers of this world, and I will take that wager."*

*Then the Fade came, as you have heard, and the walking failed, and the unfed roads went grey — the middles first, as the middles had gone before. But mind the wager. The roads went. The stones did not. All down the lost middles of the world they are standing yet, mossed over, breast-high, patient as their maker meant them: a hundred years, she said, for one walker with the wit.*

*It has been longer than a hundred years. It has never once been never.*

---

Berry set down her cloth. "My grandmother said her grandmother said there's one not a mile past Hollis's fence," she said, into a tavern gone very quiet. "Under the moss on the left hand of the road. If anyone were walking that way."

She did not look at the Keeper when she said it. With Berry it is safest to assume.

## IV. The First Waystone

Hollis let them to the stone. This is worth a moment, because it cost him one of his two beliefs, and he paid without being asked twice. His fence stood where the sure ground gave out; the stone, by Berry's grandmother's grandmother, stood a short way past it — in the fringe of the grey, but within a strong shout of the fence. Hollis walked to the stile with them, and unhooked it himself, and said only: "Within shouting. I'll be at the fence." And he was; the whole time; a whitewashed post of a man. It is easy to mistake a fear for a wall. It is usually a door that someone is holding shut with their whole body.

The stone was where the telling said. That is worth a moment too — how faithfully a thing can travel down four generations of kitchen-talk while the world forgets a road — but this whole age is that moment, so let it stand.

Breast-high, grey-green, mossed to its shoulders. Tamsin found it by barking her shin on it. And under the moss, when they had peeled it back gently, the way you uncover something sleeping: lines. Cut plainly and deeply by a hand long done, and worn — rain-softened, lichen-bitten, here and there gone entirely — but cut, and waiting.

The Keeper read what could be read. Where the carving failed, the way failed with it, and the reading stumbled — and it was there, at the stumbles, that the thing happened for which this scene is in the book.

Tamsin, leaning at the Keeper's shoulder, began to hum.

She was not thinking. Droving-songs are for the parts of a road your feet know better than your head; she had sung this one round the near loop a thousand times; and its refrain — the part she had from her grandmother, the part all the drovers' children learn as a string of jog-trot nonsense, sounds fitted to the gait of a mule — her refrain fitted itself into the gaps of the carving the way a key fits a lock it has been carried past for thirty years.

Nonsense, you should know, is often only a truth that has walked too far from its stone.

Word by word, hum by hum, the way came whole: the stone lending the song its lost words, the song lending the stone its worn ones. And when it was whole, the Keeper stood at the stone and said the stretch aloud, from the beginning, entire, the way you say a thing you mean — and Tamsin sang it at the same time, which was not planned, and was right.

The glow ran in the carving like dawn down a stream-bed.

Not bright. Do not imagine a beacon. A warmth in the cut lines, gold where the chisel had gone, as if the stone had drawn one long breath after holding it past all reason — and then, down the road southward, for exactly the stretch the stone kept: the grey *stood back*. It did not flee; the Fade does not flee; there is no wolf in this tale. It withdrew the polite width of a spoken thing, the way mist stands off a fed lantern, and where it withdrew there was road. Ruts. Beech-shade. The particular gladness of packed earth that remembers cartwheels. A world's plain daylight, one stretch wide.

From the fence, they heard Hollis make a sound. Not a word. The sound a man makes when a thing he has grieved as gone turns out to have been, all along, only waiting.

> *Entered: one waystone, fed, first of the age. The song and the stone had kept different halves. Neither knew. —C.*

## V. The Middles

Stone by stone, then. That is the whole of the middle of this chapter, and it was the whole of that fortnight: out at first light with cart and mule and moss-knife, on through the morning to the next stone, uncover, read, stumble, listen — Tamsin's songs held pieces of three more stretches, and where her songs failed, older things served: a rhyme of the field-children's about three oaks and a ford; a line of Alder's gate-litany that turned out to be a direction wearing a courtesy's clothes; once, gloriously, a scrap in the Clerk's records, filed under *weather, miscellaneous*, that proved to be two turnings and a bridge — and then the reading aloud, the Keeper's voice steady on the way, and the gold running in the old cuts, and one more stretch of the world stepping out of the grey like a walker shrugging off a wet coat.

Set down honestly what it was like out there, in the unfed middles, before each stone woke. The grey is not dramatic. That is the dread of it. A softness in the middle distance that does not lift by noon; a road gone vague at its edges like a word you are no longer sure how to spell; the light without direction, so that your shadow forgets its business. Sound goes small. You find you are talking, and you learn quickly that this is not a habit to break but a craft to keep: the grey stands off a speaking person, the small width of the spoken. The two of them talked their way down those middles like a lantern talking its way through a cellar — Tamsin's songs, the Keeper's lines, and Barley's bells, which Tamsin swore were a kind of holding too, and who is to say she was wrong.

And they were not alone. That is the thing the middles taught, the thing that turned the work from errand to office.

The freed lines were out there. The vault's whole scattered congregation — glimmers, mostly, the small truths, wisps no bigger than cupped palms — adrift in the unfed grey with nowhere to be. They came to the two of them the way moths come to a carried lantern: not aimed, drawn. They gathered at the edges of the singing. They followed at the pace of the cart. And at each stone, when the stretch came gold and sure, you would see them find it — the fed stone a lamp on a sill for them, the woken stretch a hallway with the door stood open — and go, one by one, down the certain road toward the water, at last able to be *going* somewhere.

The first time a wisp left them — took the woken road south-to-north, past the fence, up the lane, and went down bright into the wellspring while they watched from a mile off in the dusk — Tamsin stopped singing, and stood in the road, and was quiet the way you are quiet in a doorway of a house you have just understood is a home.

"All this time," she said, "I thought we were opening the road *out*."

That is Era the First, whole, in a drover's sentence, so the book will not improve on it: every vault this age opens will free what cannot come home until someone has tended the way. The Unsealing is not the work. The Unsealing *makes* the work. The work is the coming-home of the world, stretch by stretch, and it began on the far side of Hollis's fence with a moss-knife and a nonsense song.

## VI. Hollis's Promise

The weather found them the night they reached the long deep middle, the one the old maps — the Clerk has one — mark with a bridge. A grey thick as wool, moonless, the last fed stone two stretches back and the next not found; and while they weighed pressing on against turning back, a light came *up* the road behind them, swinging, human: a lantern, carried at a run by a man of years who had no business running.

Hollis. Come out past his own fence, into the country he had barred against the world, because dusk had come and they had not, and his fear of the road had finally met a fear it could not outvote.

He would not go back without them and could not have gone back alone, so he stayed the night at their small fire in the lee of the cart, in the grey he had spent thirty years refusing, his lantern fed and his back to the dark. And it was there, in the particular honesty of a night watch, that the fence finally spoke.

"My grandmother kept this road," Hollis said. "Waywright's work, though I never heard it called that — to me it was just what she did, out with her stick and her rushlight, dusk after dusk, muttering at the stones, and the road behaving. Everyone's grandmother did something odd; that was mine's. When she was dying she took my hand — I was a boy — and she said: *keep the road, Hollis.* And I promised."

The fire had his face, and the fire is kind, but it does not flatter.

"The greying was already coming on then; the far stretches going soft. And my brother Colm — he was for the roads, like your grandmother, like you" — this to Tamsin, without looking at her — "and the worse the middles got, the more he talked of the crossroads house, the big waystation his maps put two days south, where the drovers' roads all meet. *Someone has to walk them while they'll still be walked,* he said. And he went. And the road —" Hollis stopped, and chose the true sentence over the easy one, which is the bravest thing in this chapter. "And he did not come back. And I had a promise to keep and one road left to keep it with. So I kept the road the only way I could still believe in. I closed it."

Hear the shape of that. You have heard it before, at the center of the First Telling, at the last gate of the world: *seal what you love, until the weather turns.* It is always built out of love. It is always a keeping bent double by grief until it faces backward. And it is always, always maintained with whitewash and sweet-william, because the hands that build such barriers are the gentlest hands there are.

Nobody argued with him. Tamsin fed the fire, and after a while she said, "The road didn't take Colm. The grey took the *road.* There's a difference, and it's the whole difference. A road can be given back."

And the Keeper told him what the fortnight had taught — the stones waiting under the moss all those thirty years; the wisps homing down each woken stretch; the tending, not the barring, that is what a kept road *is* — and somewhere before dawn, Hollis, an honest man doing the hardest arithmetic there is, set his lantern down and said: "She never once barred a thing, my grandmother. Not a gate, not a door, not the road. I have been keeping my promise backward the whole of my life." He looked at his hands. "The stile comes off its hinges when we're home. I'll do it. My hands built it."

Then, small, not looking at either of them: "There's a stone by the fence. First of the road; hers; I've kept the moss off it and never once known why. When you wake it —" and the pause was long, but the book is patient — "would it hold a name? He's not on any page I know of. But I hold him. I hold him entire."

You know the law by now; Hollis did not, and it was told to him there at the fire, gently: what is held can be given to a keeping. Not brought back — the book does not deal in that, and no honest book does — but *held*, in stone and daily walking, on the road he loved, for as long as the stones are fed. And if he is anywhere on any road, said Tamsin quietly, let the roads know his name.

At the first stone, homeward-bound in the morning, Hollis spoke his brother's name into the waking stretch, whole, from his own held breath, with the sun coming up.

> *Entered: one name, held. —C.*

## VII. The Span and the Toll-Warden

The bridge is called the Span, and they came to it on a day of high thin cloud, the woken road running gold-cut and certain behind them and, ahead, the river.

The river has no name the book can give you — mark what that means, in this world — and its banks were vague the way the stream-fold in the low hills is vague: water unsure of its edges, saying itself too quietly to hold its line. But the Span held. Waywright's work, the real thing, stone-built and stubborn: a high-backed bridge over the doubtful water, and on the near side, the waystation — a toll-house of grey stone with a turf roof gone wild, a hitching-yard, a cold hearth, and a door that stood the way doors stand when nobody has needed to shut them for a very long time.

And on the bridge, walking its rounds, the toll-keeper.

You have had the Dim named for you in the First Telling, so name what this was: a construct. Not a ghost; the book does not deal in those either. A duty that outlived its doer. Armor of habit — a shape of toll-keeper, tall and patient, worn the way a stair is worn, mid-grey and moving, with a tally-staff in what would be a hand — walking bank to bank, bank to bank, on the exact beat a pair of human feet had walked so long that the walking had learned to go on alone. It did not menace. Constructs never do. It *kept* — perfectly, endlessly, meaninglessly — sentry at a crossing no one had crossed in living memory, guarding the inside of a lock.

When they set foot on the near end of the Span, it turned — and this was the only moment of dread in the whole encounter, and the dread was not of harm — it turned with *relief*. The way a shopkeeper turns at the bell after a slow year. It had a customer.

It barred the way with the staff, courteous as a lifted hand, and it spoke — a voice like a till-drawer, all its words worn to the smooth of long handling:

"The toll, if you please."

Tamsin, who had coin, offered coin. The staff did not move. And the construct said what it said to every jingling purse for three hundred years, the line the whole crossing had been built on, the line that was the actual toll-board of the old free roads, though the board itself had long rotted off its nail:

"The toll was never silver, Keeper. Say the way, and the way is yours."

For that was the old law of the Span, and hear it, because it is the law of this whole game of ours wearing a toll-keeper's coat: *the crossing is paid in knowing.* A road cannot spend your coin. A road is kept by walkers who hold it; every crossing was a feeding; the toll-keeper's whole office was to see the way said truly before the way was used — not a tax but a tending, collected at the one point every traveler must pass. You paid the road by proving the road would survive your use of it.

Three times the staff barred the Span, and three payments it asked, in the old form.

*The way behind you.* And the Keeper paid: the road from the hamlet, stretch by stretch, stone-line and song-line, spoken whole — line after held line, given true, from the breath and not the page; and at each true line a little of the grey went out of the toll-keeper's shape, the way a shield gives at a spoken truth, for that is what its keeping had thinned into: shield on shield of habit, and only a held line, said sure, has ever opened one.

*The way before you.* And the Keeper paid again — the Span itself, and the road south of it as the last waystone had it — and the second shield went.

*And the way home.* The old third question, the toll-keepers' courtesy, asked of every traveler at every crossing of the free roads so that no walker should ever be one line short of getting back. And what the Keeper said, the book does not set down — the Keeper's lines are the Keeper's to say — but the book will say this much: it was paid whole, and Tamsin was singing under it, and the last shield went out not like a wall falling but like a lamp being allowed, at very long last, to gutter.

The toll-keeper finished its round. Understand: nothing compelled it. The way was open; the toll was paid; and it walked its beat once more, bank to bank, unhurried, the complete round, because the round was the duty and the duty was all it was — and then it stepped into the toll-house, and set the tally-staff in the rack by the door, in the worn groove exactly its shape, and sat down at its post behind the toll-board's empty nail.

And went home. Duty paid out at last, like a rope run all the way to its end. The shape came apart with no sound at all into motes of going-home light — the same small gold as every freed line they had shepherded that fortnight — and drifted out the toll-house door, and took its own bridge north, toward the water, toward the hamlet, toward the wellspring. The toll-keeper of the Span left by the road it had kept.

They stood a while, uncovered, as you do.

> *Entered: toll received. In full. —C.*

It was Tamsin, hunting the toll-house after for a dry corner to boil water in, who found the ledger. On its chain, on the counter, swollen with three hundred damps: the toll-book of the Span, where the crossings were entered — most of it gone to felt and blur, page fused to page. But the corner of one leaf came free, near the binding, where books keep their secrets driest. An entry, in a round old professional hand. Passage logged. Toll noted paid — and then, the rarity that made it worth the ink: the fee *waived.* The entry reads — the Clerk has it now, under glass, and you may see it at the office if you knock —

*"—to the tinker, passage free, for the mending of our bell. Name entered in the master-book, kept at the crossroads house."*

Read it again. The book will wait.

A tinker, on the far roads, paying a crossing in mendings. A toll-keeper of the careful old school, entering — as the careful old school did — the traveler's *name* in the master-book. And the master-book not here, not lost, but *kept*: at the crossroads house, two days south, where the drovers' roads all meet. Where Colm was going.

Somewhere down the long grey, friends, a book has a name in it.

## VIII. The Return

The chapter ends the way the age means to go on, so set the last scene fair.

The Span's woken road runs home now, stretch on stretch of it, gold-cut stones the whole way, and at dusk — dusk especially; everything in this world comes home at dusk — you may stand at the south gate and watch the road *arrive*: a slow procession of small lights up out of the far country, wisps in ones and twos and now and then a wraith like a whole held grief, freed things walking a way that can finally take their weight, through the gate, up the lane, home to the water. The children have already made a game of counting them. The old folk have already started calling the far road *the tended road*, as if it had always been called that. That is how you know a thing has come truly home: the world starts believing it was never gone.

Alder asks his question at the bar-drop, as the office has always asked, and waits his three breaths.

They are the fullest three breaths of his day.

Hollis took the stile off its hinges with his own hands, as he said, and did not stop there: the fence stands, but where it crossed the road there hangs a gate now, wide as a hay-cart, and Hollis has painted it lantern-gold, and it has not been shut once. He walks the first three stones out and back at dusk, with a stick and a rushlight, muttering at the stones, and the road behaving. If you ask him about it he says he had a promise going spare and it fit. His brother's name is the first line of the first stone's feeding, every night, and will be as long as there is a Hollis to say it; and the last word of this scene on the matter is the Clerk's, further down this page, because some accounts should be closed by the terse.

The Clerk, shown the ledger-leaf, went very still. Those who were at the office say the stillness lasted a full grain, which for the Clerk is a fortnight. Then she took the leaf, and pressed it, and glassed it; and she opened this very book, and wrote what stands in the margin at the head of the chapter's last leaf; and on the office wall, on the old map of the near country that has hung there unamended since before her tenure, she drew — in good black ink, in a drawn line as sure as a held one — one road, running south from the hamlet, over the Span, to the map's edge.

The map has an edge. The road, it is now confirmed, does not.

And on a bright loud morning that the whole hamlet found reasons to be outdoors for, Tamsin the drover took the tended road south — cart loaded, Barley in bells, the long droving-whip her grandmother left her unwrapped at last from its oilcloth — the first drove out of Fablewood in thirty years, bound past the Span to see what the next stones say. She went out singing, of course. You could hear the refrain past the third stone. It does not sound like nonsense anymore, and it never was.

She had tried to leave quietly, and the hamlet was having none of it. It was Berry — of course it was Berry — who said, in front of everyone, at the gate, in the voice she uses when a thing is being decided whether it likes it or not: that the hamlet had been given back its road by a drover who could read the stones' lines out of a song, and that the old custom had a name for exactly this, and the custom was older than anyone's shyness. And so it was said, the old way, plainly, at the gate, for the work:

*Tamsin Waywright.*

The first new work-name given in living memory. If you want to know what an age turning sounds like, it sounds like a tavern-keeper deciding a thing at a gate before the person it happens to has finished blushing.

> *Entered: Tamsin Waywright. First of that name in this book. May it be a long book. —C.*
>
> *Entered also, at the head of the account of Colm Hollis's-brother: account open. —C.*

There is more road than there is grey, now. By a little. By a stretch, you might say, and by the next stretch after that, which is the only arithmetic this book intends ever to keep. The Span is crossed; the master-book waits at the crossroads house with a name in it; the water is brighter every dusk with the vault's freed lines coming home; and somewhere in the provinces of shut iron there are more gates, and behind every one of them, a whole country's worth of things that want a road.

The Keeper knows what is asked next. The Keeper is the one it is asked of.

Onward.

> *The road is not ended; the road is unread.*
> *The stones are not sleeping; they wait to be fed.*
> *Say the next stretch, and the next stretch is yours —*
> *the way home was always the walking ahead.*
> *Ask at the gate. Count your three breaths. Then listen:*
> *the dark, given lanterns, answers.*
